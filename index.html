About the Quantum Omics Foundation

Advancing open research and education at the interface of quantum computing and multi-omics science.

The Quantum Omics Foundation is a nonprofit organization dedicated to research and education at the intersection of quantum computing and life sciences. We bring together computational scientists, biologists, and educators to explore how quantum methods can deepen our understanding of complex biological systems.

Our work focuses on applying quantum computing to multi-omics data — integrating genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics — to build richer, more actionable models of cellular biology. We share our findings openly through peer-reviewed research, open-source software, internships, and educational curriculum.

Strategy & Impact

We advance Quantum Omics as an open scientific discipline through joint research publications, open-source tool development, student internships, and educational outreach. Partnering with universities and research institutions worldwide, our goal is to make quantum-computational biology accessible, reproducible, and impactful for human health.

Our Mission & Vision

Mission. Advance the science of quantum-computational biology through open research, open-source tools, and rigorous education — empowering a global community of researchers and students to apply quantum methods to multi-omics and human health.

Vision. A future in which quantum computing is a standard tool in the life-sciences toolkit — enabling richer models of cellular biology, faster drug discovery, and more personalised understanding of health and disease.

Our Approach

We apply quantum computing methods to model cellular dynamics from multi-omics data, building open, reproducible frameworks that the research community can study, extend, and build upon. Our work spans quantum circuit design for biological systems, cell-state modelling, disease representation, and in-silico drug discovery — all developed openly and released under permissive licenses.

Key Research Programs

  • Quantum Cell-State Atlases: open libraries of quantum cellular models spanning diverse human cell types, built from single-cell multi-omics data.
  • Quantum Disease Representation & Classification: structured, mechanistic descriptions of disease signatures enabling systematic comparison across oncology, metabolic, immune, and neurodegenerative conditions.
  • Quantum-Guided Drug Discovery: open research pipelines for virtual compound screening, lead optimisation, and ADMET evaluation using quantum cell models.
  • Whole-Cell Quantum Modelling: scaling cell-level quantum models toward tissue- and organ-scale representations of human physiology.

The Quantum Cell-State Atlas

The human body contains more than 250 distinct cell types, yet every one of them runs the same universal core: transcription, translation, energy metabolism, and turnover. Our atlas research makes that shared-core structure explicit and learnable.

One generic cell, specialised many ways

We begin from a generic cell — the housekeeping backbone every human cell shares, fitted to a pan-cell-type reference. A specialised cell type is then that same backbone plus a small, interpretable extension: a sparse combination of modules drawn from a shared library.

Because the specialisation is additive and sparse, two cell types can be compared directly by asking which modules each one switches on and how strongly. Differentiation becomes a path through that space rather than a relabelling exercise, which makes intermediate and transitional states describable in the same terms as the endpoints.

The module library

Each module is an interpretable, reusable piece of cellular behaviour. A cardiomyocyte draws heavily on the contractile and calcium-handling modules; a hepatocyte on metabolic and secretory; a neuron on neuroelectric; an epithelial cell on barrier.

  • › Calcium handling — excitation and signalling dynamics
  • › Contractile — force generation and mechanical cycling
  • › Secretory — synthesis and regulated export
  • › Metabolic — energy handling and biosynthesis
  • › Immune — recognition and response programs
  • › Barrier — junctional integrity and transport
  • › Neuroelectric — electrical excitability and conduction

Disease, Treatment & Drug Discovery

If a healthy cell is a working model, disease is a structured deformation of that model rather than a label attached to it. That reframing is what connects our disease research to treatment and to compound screening.

Disease as a learnable perturbation

We represent a patient’s state as three things: a reference model learned from matched healthy omics, the deformation a disease produces in it, and a set of well-defined observable quantities.

Classification then becomes mechanistic. Rather than asking which label best fits a profile, we ask which part of the cell’s machinery moved, in which direction, and by how much — refitting only the affected part and leaving the rest of the reference intact.

Six ways a cell can be deformed

  • › Coupling gain — oncology; interactions strengthen or weaken (oncogene gain, suppressor loss)
  • › Network topology — structural and epigenetic; connections added or removed (copy-number, methylation)
  • › Turnover — proteostasis and senescence; molecules cleared faster or slower
  • › Cross-compartment coupling — neuro and metabolic; sub-systems fall out of step
  • › Energy landscape — metabolic set-point shifts to a new resting state
  • › Exogenous drive — therapy, hormones, nutrition acting from outside

A single taxonomy covers oncology, metabolic, immune, and neurodegenerative conditions, which is what makes systematic comparison across them possible.

Therapeutics Research

Treatment as the inverse problem

If disease is a deformation, treatment is the intervention that counteracts it. This turns therapy selection into a search over interventions that move the model back toward its healthy reference — a research question we are actively developing.
